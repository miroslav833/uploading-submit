## Summit pre-submission checklist

Go through every line before you report a task as ready. Each one has cost a returned version.

1. **Difficulty.** Key feature plainly visible; difficulty in the interpretation. Local Opus about 2 of 3 over 4
   or more runs, Sonnet at most 1 of 3, no-image run wrong, hint run on the key. 0 of n locally comes back
   "very hard"; a quick intended-route solve comes back "too easy".
2. **Image quality.** Every measured or counted feature is unambiguous at the size a reviewer sees it, with no
   look-alike debris or artifact next to it. task214 came back from a human reviewer (code I2): rods and tips too
   faint to identify or measure, distal dots that looked like stain artifacts.
3. **AI authorship.** Write tersely: uneven step lengths, no closing recap, rubric lines of different shapes,
   do not explain every wrong answer, no stock phrasing. task201 v3 and v4 and task222 v2 failed the check;
   task217 v2 and task218 v2 passed (read their fields/ for the voice).
4. **Consistency check.**
   (a) Every range or rounding claim in the solution and verify box is arithmetically true; compute each one
   with a script and quote its output. task217 v4 failed on "0.124 to 0.160 rounds to 70" when the ends give 60
   and 80.
   (b) No single plausible mistake, and no pair of them, lands on the key at the stated precision. task222
   failed because a fat-line misreading gave 26 %, which rounds to the key 30.
   (c) Define every symbol and convention in the stem. task219 failed over outer against mid-surface radius.
   (d) Sources: Original whenever the key comes from the figure. task228 failed with Published source.
   (e) The precision field matches the key's significant figures. task221 has key -1.17 but precision 2 s.f.
5. **Expert difficulty.** The one hard step is something a PhD-level specialist knows and a strong final-year
   undergraduate does not. Cite the specialist literature for it. task217 v5 was rejected because its hard step
   (Fraser-Lee back-calculation from a scale) is standard undergraduate lab material.
6. **Duplicates.** Check note-chemistry.md, note.md and the web before building. task200 v2 was refused as
   matching an earlier submission; the same quantity on the same kind of data is a duplicate.
7. **Category.** Build Chemistry tasks; most needs-revision slots being replaced are Chemistry.
8. **Form.** Import .zip leaves the verify box empty (paste fields/11-verify-box.txt). Set by hand: label
   Knowledge (or Reasoning as the README says), image rights "I made it for this question", and the precision.

## 1. Pick a family that can land in the band

Most designs fail by being too easy, and the new rules make that side harder, not easier. Opus now runs at
extra-high effort, and every tally in the logs was measured at high effort, where Summit's runs were already
longer and stronger than local ones. Under the old rules the platform's real band was also narrower than the
written one (returned tasks showed "band 1-3" and a 4 of 5 came back too easy); whether 0 to 5 of 8 is applied
as written is not known yet. The bottom is not open after all: local 0 of n came back "very hard" on five tasks
(2026-11-18), so design for Opus right on about 2 of 3, with a plainly visible feature whose interpretation it
sometimes gets wrong, and still spend real care on the key, the proof and a solution a reader can follow.

Build image tasks. The user confirmed on 2026-11-15 that the text-only-only notice of 2026-10-21 is the old
rule and the current instruction is the image version. Text-only is still allowed by the instruction, but do
not choose it unless the user asks: eight text-only chemistry families were piloted (ch30, ch31) and Opus was
right on all 15 runs at high effort, and text-only is easier to search. Every chemistry pass so far rested on
reading a figure. An image task now meets the no-image check, so nothing in the stem, the literature prior or
a short list of guessable values may give the key away (see the weaker-model point below, which applies to
the no-image runs too).

Keep the topic plainly chemistry. The user does not want biology tasks, because Opus 5.5 often refused them
as "bio", and a refused run is no answer, locally or at Summit. The same can happen to a chemistry stem that
reads like biology or medicine (pathogens, toxins, drugs and dosing, human or animal physiology), so prefer
materials, surfaces, physical, inorganic, polymer and physical organic chemistry, and if a blind run refuses,
change the framing or the family instead of rerunning.

These lessons come from more than a hundred biology tasks and hold for any domain:

- **Always solved.** Anything that reduces to a known theorem or formula plus a finite search or a clean
  derivation. Opus 5.5 retrieves the method and runs it by hand, even when the search is large. In chemistry,
  expect the same for textbook machinery: point groups and IR/Raman counts, term symbols, electron counting,
  Woodward-Hoffmann and FMO selection rules, CIP assignment, stereoisomer counts, Hückel for small rings,
  first-order NMR analysis, standard kinetics linearisations and named-reaction outcomes.
- **Clean figures are read exactly.** Both models read bar heights, peak positions on a labelled axis and
  integrals to about 1 percent. A drawn structure is parsed atom by atom. Perception alone is a weak lever, and
  where it works it is all-or-nothing: the same figure has gone 0 of 5 at one view size and 5 of 5 at another.
- **Recall levers fail.** Hiding the key behind a convention or a fact that Opus might misremember (a sign
  convention, a reading rule) went 5 of 5 at Summit even when local runs at lower effort missed it.
- **Search volume is not difficulty.** A run cut off at the output limit is not a miss any more: one cut-off
  is tolerated and counted as right, and two of the eight return the task. Extra-high effort also spends more
  output per run than the high-effort runs in the logs did (those were cut off after about 32 to 40 minutes).
  The difficulty has to sit in one decisive step that a run reaches in a few thousand tokens, and a local run
  that works for a very long time is a warning even when its answer is wrong.
- **A program is not difficulty.** If a short program, a simulation or a brute-force search gives the answer,
  the instruction calls the question too easy, whatever the blind runs did by hand. Code belongs in the
  verification notes, to check what the written steps derive. A key that only an enumeration can certify fails
  this as well as the proof rule in section 3.
- **What has reached the band.** One decisive step where the natural method gives a plausible wrong answer
  (a bound mistaken for the value, a standard count that overshoots or undershoots by one, the commonest case
  taken when the figure shows the rarer one), decided by a feature that is in the figure but easy to pass over.
  The chemistry log starts with ch01: the Longuet-Higgins star count gives one NBMO, the true count is three,
  because a concealed non-Kekulé region hides two more (still too easy: Opus ran the zero-sum rule).
  **First chemistry pass at Summit: ch03** (tasks-chemistry/ch03-porphyrin-nanoring-exciton). An exact,
  easy derivation (dark-to-bright Frenkel gap of a porphyrin nanoring, 4|V| sin²(π/N)) whose only input is
  an integer N read from a noisy simulated STM image of irregular, flexible rings. Irregular shapes make the
  contour-length shortcut give a range, not the value; a famous literature size (c-P30) one step from the key
  (N = 32) catches a hedging solver; the contrast between neighbouring units was tuned with Opus pilots
  between "counts directly" and "falls back to the prior", with a PNG readback script proving every ring stays
  countable. Pure reasoning families (ch01 to ch03 pilots) all went to Opus quickly; start new designs from
  this shape. It was then returned for findability (see the next-but-one point), so pair the perception step
  with a derivation that is not a printed formula. Details in note-chemistry.md.
- **Weaker-model probe and no-image check.** Sonnet now may not be right even once in three tries, and for an
  image question neither may the three runs that never see the image. If the key equals the literature prior
  or a lucky guess, either one gets it. List the answers a solver would guess, not only the ones a wrong method
  produces, and keep the key off that list. Small answer spaces are the danger: a count between 5 and 9, a
  yes/no, one of four labels. Prefer keys with many plausible values (a number to three figures that depends on
  every reading, a sequence, a multi-part label).
- **Duplicates and findability.** The same quantity on the same kind of data is a duplicate even with new
  framing, and textbook classics are public. Search the web for the stem and the key before building.
  Findability also covers the derivation: ch03 v2 passed the band and was still returned because, after
  counting N in the image, the answer was a published closed form with N substituted (ring Bloch states,
  k = ±1 bright). The reasoning after the perception step needs one step the sources do not print; ch03 v3
  kept the figure and asked for the lowest two-exciton state instead (hard-core excitons, antiperiodic
  Jordan-Wigner fermions).
- **Graduate level.** If it is taught in standard undergraduate courses (introductory organic chemistry
  included), it does not qualify, however long the calculation.
- **Stable.** The key may not depend on a library or program version, hardware or a random seed, nor on a
  result that exists only in very recent papers. A simulated figure is fixed once rendered, so this concerns
  what the solver must compute or know, and any value the solver needs is quoted in the stem.
- **Determinable.** The figure plus the stem must fix the key. A crystallography task (bio task50) failed the
  consistency check because "blank in the figure" did not prove a systematic absence; state such conventions
  in the stem.

Design the trap first: the one step where the natural approach gives a plausible wrong answer. Then choose
parameters so the key and every trap answer differ at the requested precision. Untested chemistry directions
that fit this shape, to pilot rather than trust: second-order NMR multiplets where a first-order reading gives
the wrong J or shift (key from the exact spin Hamiltonian); kinetics or Eyring plots where the obvious
linearisation picks the wrong regime; cyclic voltammograms where the mechanism (EC, adsorption) changes what a
peak means; diffraction patterns with an accidental near-absence; graph-theoretic counts (Kekulé structures,
NBMOs, Clar sextets) where the standard bound is not the value. Record every family you try in note-chemistry.md,
because that log is the main defence against repeating a dead one.

## 3. Build it

Folder layout (one task per folder):

```
tasks-chemistry/chNN-slug/
  spec.py            all package text and settings (written by new_chem_task.py from the template)
  question.txt       the stem, the only copy (the blind test uses this file), 3,000 characters at most
  image_source.py    or image_source.tex: draws image.png and image.pdf (keep the source); image tasks only
  verification/      scripts that make the data and recompute the key, data files
  probes/            pilot stems, figures and RESULTS.md
  reference.txt  task.json  README.md  fields/  <SLUG>.zip      written by build_package.py
```

- **Key.** Know it by construction and recompute it by at least one independent route that sees only what the
  solver sees; ideally also read it back from the PNG. Compute every common wrong answer by running the wrong
  method. Prefer keys that are exact (counts, fractions, symmetry labels, structures) or follow from stated data
  with a closed form. If a key depends on a computed property (an energy, a shift), fix the level of theory or
  the parameter set in the stem, and know how much the key moves under reasonable alternatives.
- **Proofs.** A key that is a minimum or maximum needs a hand-checkable argument or certificate in the solution
  and the notes (ch01 used a cut through the graph plus an explicit matching), not just search output. Summit's
  consistency check blocked a task whose lower bound rested on "the search found nothing better".
- **Chemical realism.** A chemist reviews this. Every structure must pass RDKit sanitisation, exist or be
  plausible (check strain and steric clashes; ch01 filtered drawn PAHs by perimeter H...H distances), and every
  simulated spectrum or plot must use realistic values: shift ranges, coupling constants, line widths, rate
  constants, noise. Units SI unless the field convention differs, and then say which.
- **Scripts.** Use the standard library where you can. Summit's consistency check reads only the uploaded
  notes, so build_package.py embeds each script's full source, command and output. Every number the solution
  quotes must appear in some script's printed output.
- **Text-only.** Set MODALITY "text_only", TASK_TYPE "not_applicable" and FIGURE_CMD = None; the zip then
  holds task.json and reference.txt. Everything is in the stem: structures as SMILES or IUPAC names, spectra as
  peak lists, data as a small table, all inside the 3,000 characters. Search the web for the stem and the key
  with extra care.
- **Figure (only when the question cannot be answered without it).** Made with code, never copied, never a screenshot of text, never a spectrum taken from a database.
  Everything the solver needs is in the figure and nowhere in the stem. Tools:
  - RDKit (`rdMolDraw2D.MolDraw2DCairo`, 2000 px or more) for structures: explicit wedges and hashes for
    stereochemistry, atom numbers only where the question refers to atoms, never CIP labels (they give answers
    away; `addStereoAnnotation` stays False).
  - chemfig or TikZ through `scripts/render_tex.py` (FIGURE_CMD `["{std}", "{scripts}/render_tex.py",
    "image_source.tex"]`) for mechanisms and schemes; chemfig angles go wrong easily, so check every bond.
  - matplotlib for spectra, kinetics, voltammograms and diffraction patterns: labelled axes with units, ticks
    a reader can use.
  Look at the PNG yourself (Read it, crop regions) for label collisions, cut-off text, wedges overlapping atoms
  and anything that gives the answer away, and look at an 800 px copy too (`blind_setup.py --sizes 800`).
- **Stem.** At most 3,000 characters with LaTeX and markdown counted; the builder fails above that and prints
  the count. Leave some room, because the form counts as the user types and a returned task is edited there.
  If a design needs a longer stem, move data into the figure or cut the setup; a longer question is not a
  harder one. State every assumption and convention, point at the figure, say what form the answer takes ("as a
  single integer", "in kJ mol$^{-1}$ to three significant figures"), define any non-standard term. Single
  answer, not compound. Formulas in plain LaTeX (`$\mathrm{C_{53}H_{25}}$`), never mhchem `\ce{}`, because
  Summit's preview is not known to load it; the builder flags it.
- **Classification.** FIELD is the subdomain key written to task.json as `field`: `organic`,
  `physical_computational`, `materials_polymer_catalysis`, `inorganic_coordination` or `chemistry_other`
  (then SUBDOMAIN names it). SUBDOMAIN is the raw subject, the specific topic. LABEL is "reasoning" (the answer
  comes from working the problem) or "knowledge" (it needs a specialist fact or method); the zip has no key for
  it, so the user ticks it on the form and the README shows which.
- **References.** Peer-reviewed journals or proceedings only. No preprints that were not peer reviewed, no
  textbooks, lecture notes, blogs or Wikipedia.
- **Rubric.** 3 to 5 criteria summing to 10. Exactly one is the final answer and states the key; give it the
  most points. The others are checkable intermediate results, method-neutral ("by any valid method"), each a
  yes/no that two graders would agree on. Never "explains clearly" or "attempts". Name the most tempting wrong
  answer in the solution, in the verify box and first in the wrong answers, but never in a rubric criterion:
  Summit's consistency check blocked a criterion that judged which error was most tempting. List at most 10
  common wrong answers: the form takes no more (user, 2026-11-15; ch37 imported 12). Keep the ones a wrong
  method gives and the likeliest single misreads; the builder fails above 10.

Fill spec.py (worked example: `tasks-chemistry/ch01-abx-second-order-coupling/v0-nbmo-benzenoid/spec.py`; read it, do not edit it),
then:

```bash
python3 .claude/skills/summit-chemistry-task/scripts/build_package.py tasks-chemistry/chNN-slug
```

It runs the figure and verification scripts, writes reference.txt, task.json, fields/, README.md and the zip,
validates Summit's rules (points, a final-answer criterion that contains the key as a whole token, wrong
answers, precision wording, the 3,000-character question limit, FIELD and LABEL, sizes, task type, no mhchem)
and runs the tells detector. A spec written before 2026-11-15 has no FIELD or LABEL and fails until both are
added. It prints the final-answer
criterion; read it by eye, because a one-digit key can still sit in the wrong place. Fix everything it prints and
rebuild.

## 4. Write like a person

All submitted text has to read as written by a researcher: stem, solution, rubric, image-necessity box,
references, verify box. Follow humanism.md. No em or en dashes, no "Note that", no scaffold labels, no
therefore/thus/moreover/crucial/robust, no bullet-list solutions, no remarks about AI models or about the
question itself. The numbered "Step N:" format is required; each step is ordinary prose. The user dislikes the
bold-lead-in-plus-colon pattern. In rubric criteria fix only dashes and quotes, because that wording is graded.
`scripts/humancheck.py` must print CLEAN before the screen.

Summit now runs its own AI-writing check on the question, the solution and the rubric before the form will
submit, and asks for a rewrite in the user's own words when the score is over its threshold. humancheck CLEAN
is the least that is needed, not a promise of passing. Say in the report that this gate exists; if it stops a
task, the user rewrites the flagged field and the summit-draft-refine skill brings the rewrite back to the
task's exact meaning.

The verify box is read by a reviewer who may not open the attached notes and cannot copy data out of a figure.
When the figure is the only source of the data, the verify box must contain:
- the data as text (SMILES or ring positions, peak lists, data points);
- the key worked by hand;
- the most tempting wrong answer, worked the same way;
- a few-line standard-library program that checks the key in seconds.

A biology task was rejected when two Summit runs converged on a wrong answer and the reviewer, with no data in
text, took that as proof of a wrong key. Write "the most tempting wrong answer", never a remark about model runs.
If the user brings their own wording of a field, the summit-draft-refine skill handles it; rebuild with this
skill's build_package.py afterwards.

## 5. Blind screen: Opus 5.5 extra-high, Sonnet 5, no image, solution as hint

Screen only the final stem and figure. A screen run before the last stem or figure edit is stale; rerun it. Set
up an isolated, randomly named folder in the session scratchpad, so the solvers never see the task folder
(build first, the hint folder takes the solution from fields/):

```bash
~/.venvs/chem/bin/python .claude/skills/summit-chemistry-task/scripts/blind_setup.py tasks-chemistry/chNN-slug \
    --out-root <scratchpad>/blindtest --field "<subfield, e.g. physical organic chemistry>" --hint --no-image
```

(`--no-image` does nothing for a text-only task; plain `python3` is enough when `--sizes` is not used.) The
script prints one prompt per folder. Spawn all the agents in one message with the Agent tool,
`run_in_background: true`, each with its own prompt:

| Run | Agent | Mirrors | Wanted |
|---|---|---|---|
| blind, 4 or more | `opus55-solver`, `effort: "xhigh"` | the 8 tries | right on about 2 of 3 (2 or 3 of 4) |
| weaker | `sonnet5-solver` | the 3 Sonnet tries | wrong |
| noimage/ (image tasks) | `opus55-solver`, `effort: "xhigh"` | the 3 tries without the image | wrong |
| hint/ | `opus55-solver`, `effort: "xhigh"` | the solution-as-hint check | reaches the key, no step disputed |

Summit says extra-high reasoning effort; `xhigh` is that level here, and this skill asks for it explicitly.
A pilot of a decisive step (section 2) needs only the blind Opus run.

Each solver states its model in its last lines; a run that names another model does not count. A run that ends
with "You've hit your session limit" or was stopped by a safeguard flag is not an answer; rerun it with the same
prompt. Don't read the agents' output files; their reports arrive as notifications. Grade each final answer
against the key and note the time and the method in one line.

How to read them (target from the 2026-11-18 returns; see the checklist above):
- Blind Opus right on every run quickly by the intended route: too easy.
- Blind Opus wrong on every run: comes back "very hard" (five tasks on 2026-11-18). Make the key feature easier
  to see or the interpretation less hidden, and rescreen.
- Blind Opus right on about 2 of 3 (2 or 3 of 4), misses for a scientific reason: the wanted result.
- Blind Opus runs very long: note the time. Two cut-off tries of eight return the task.
- Sonnet right: the task fails that check as it stands (one right answer in three is enough).
- No-image run right: the image is not needed, or the key can be guessed. Find out which words gave it away.
- Hint run does not reach the key, or disputes a step: settle that before anything else. Either the key or the
  solution is wrong, or a step is missing from the solution. When the key rests on reading the figure, a hint
  run that misreads the figure the same way the blind run did is a real risk at Summit too, so the solution
  must say what is read and where (which features, which region), not only what follows from the reading.
- Summit's runs are longer and stronger than local ones, and a local 4 of 5 has come back 5 of 5, so aim lower
  than the edge.

Run more blind runs than these (or reduced-size copies) only when the user asks for it.

## 6. If the screen says too easy, or a check fails

Both directions fail: 0 of n locally comes back "very hard", a clean sweep comes back "too easy". Besides
those, the Sonnet run, the no-image run, the hint run and a run that does not finish can fail. Standing rule from the user: when there is a plausible change
that could put Opus in band or clear the failed check, make it in the same task folder (archive the old version in a subfolder such as `v1-.../`), rebuild and rescreen. When the family
has no lever left, say so plainly and stop; do not open a second task in this session without the user's word.
Record what was tried either way.

## 1. Pick a family that can land in the band

Most designs fail by being too easy. The record (details in note.md) says:

- **Always solved.** Anything that reduces to a known theorem or formula plus a finite search or clean
  derivation: coalescent, pedigree kinship and inbreeding, linkage LOD, hybridization numbers, parsimony and
  minimum evolution on hand-sized data, recombination counts, inversion distance, knot identification, standard

## Lessons and tips

- Polyhex enumeration (2026-10-19, ch01): growing free polyhexes level by level in Python with a set of canonical tuples is fast to h = 13 (3.27M shapes, about 4 min on 16 cores) but h = 14 (about 16M) ran out of the 62 GB RAM and killed the pool. For bigger h, grow only from a seed family (for example the hits of the level below) instead of the full set.
- A wait loop `until ...; ! pgrep -f run14.py` never ends because pgrep matches the loop's own command line. Wait on a PID or on a line in the log, not on pgrep -f.
- Same trap again with kill: `for p in $(pgrep -f run14.log)` also matched the shell running that loop and killed it (exit 144). Kill by explicit PID only.
- Chemistry design that maps onto the passed-task pattern (natural count plus a hidden overshoot): Hückel NBMO count of a benzenoid. The star rule n* - n0 gives only a lower bound; the true count is n - 2*nu (Fajtlowicz, John, Sachs 2005 for benzenoids), equal to the exact rank deficit of the zero-sum equations. Concealed non-Kekulé benzenoids (n* = n0, no Kekulé structure) start at h = 11 (8 shapes, all eta = 2); the odd-radical version (n* - n0 = 1, eta = 3) needs h = 14 (51 shapes found by growing the h = 12/13 concealed ones).
- Realism filter for drawn PAHs: compute idealised perimeter H...H distances (C-C 1.40 A, C-H 1.09 A along the outward bisector). Bays give 1.71 A, coves and fjords 0.5 to 0.8 A (molecule cannot be planar). Of 1137 grown candidates only 4 had bays only.
- A cut through bonds that all run unstarred-to-starred in the same direction is the hand proof of hidden NBMOs: each side keeps its own sublattice excess. The König/Hall alternating-path set from the unmatched starred carbons gives that cut automatically.
- Rubric key check in build_package.py is a substring test, so a one-digit key ("3") passes trivially. Read the final-answer criterion by eye.
- ch01 round 1 (direct question "how many NBMOs"): Opus 5.5 high answered 3, correct, in 146 s by running the zero-sum rule on both sublattices; it knew n* - n0 is only a lower bound. A direct question about a quantity with an exact mechanical method (zero-sum propagation, rank) gets computed, not guessed. Sonnet 5 misread the 15-ring skeleton as 17 rings / 60 C and used NBMOs = |nA - nB|; it took 20 min. Large PAH drawings are hard for Sonnet to read, easy for Opus.
- Lever tried for round 2: ask for a consequence (smallest closed-shell cation charge z) so the solver has to decide by itself that the NBMO degeneracy needs checking.

## Section 3: Solution

Numbered steps ("Step 1: …"), each with the formula written out, every symbol defined the first time it appears, and every intermediate number shown. Units carried through. Any assumption not already in the question gets flagged here, though it should really be moved into the question. Keep it purely scientific: no remarks about AI models. Cite peer-reviewed papers only. Field: `solution` (up to 30,000 characters).

## Section 4: Grading rubric

- Three to five criteria, points summing to exactly 10. Each criterion is one yes/no check that two graders would agree on.
- Exactly one criterion carries the **Final-answer criterion** radio button. It must state the actual answer ("Gives 0.3538"), never "gives the correct answer". Give it the most points, usually 5.
- The other criteria are specific intermediate results or decisive choices: a value, a classification, a formula applied correctly. Not "shows working", not "explains clearly".
- Common wrong answers: list the numbers a strong solver reaches by making the natural mistake, each one different from the key. Use **Add wrong answer** for each.
- `task.json`: `rubric.criteria` entries have `id`, `description`, `points`, `isFinalAnswer`; wrong answers go in `rubric.commonWrongAnswers`.

Labels applied automatically: Correct if the final-answer criterion is met; Partially correct if it is not but another criterion is; Incorrect otherwise.

## Section 5: References (optional but worth filling)

The explanation box ("Please explain where to find or verify the answer") is locked until a source file is uploaded. Upload a file first; a short text file with your own verification notes counts, as does a PDF you are allowed to share. Then write where the answer can be verified and cite the papers. In `task.json`: `referenceFile.entry` (file in the zip) and `referenceFile.fileName` (display name), plus the free-text `references`.
