The figure is the only source of the number of pinholes. It is a simulated back-lit micrograph, 232 x 232 px, made for this question. The pinholes are the bright dots on a circle of radius 79.9 px about the point (115.5, 115.5) px, 8.8 px apart. There are 57 of them, at the azimuths 1.83 degrees + k x 6.316 degrees for k = 0 to 56, counter-clockwise from the direction to the right (1.8, 8.1, 14.5, 20.8, 27.1, ...). Counted by quadrant from the right, counter-clockwise, they are 14, 15, 14 and 14.

Key by hand. N = 57 and a chord of 150.0 um give the circle radius R = 150.0/(2 sin(pi/57)) = 1361.5 um. The field in the focal plane is the sum over the pinholes of exp[i 64 phi_j - i u cos(theta - phi_j)] with u = 2 pi R r/(lambda f). Expanding in Bessel functions, only the azimuthal orders 64 + 57 q survive: ..., -50, 7, 64, 121, ... Near the axis the order 7 stands alone, so the intensity is proportional to J_7(u)^2, dark on the axis, with its first bright ring at the first maximum of J_7, u = 8.5778. Then r = 8.5778 x 632.8 nm x 500.0 mm/(2 pi x 1.3615 mm) = 0.3173 mm, which is 0.32 mm.

The most tempting wrong answer is 2.5 mm: the ring taken as continuous, a vortex of charge 64 in the focal plane and the first maximum of J_64 at u = 67.25. Other routes: 0.41 mm (first zero of J_7 in place of its maximum), 0.14 mm (the vortex ignored, first side ring of J_0, which is also the rule 1.22 lambda f/D), 0.16 mm (diameter of the circle used as its radius), 0.48 mm (second ring of J_7). Miscounts: 56 pinholes 0.36 mm, 58 pinholes 0.27 mm, 55 pinholes 0.41 mm, 59 pinholes 0.23 mm, 64 pinholes a bright centre with its first side ring at 0.13 mm. Within 12 of the true count no other number of pinholes comes closer than 14 per cent.

Check program (Python 3, standard library), a direct sum of the 57 waves:
import math, cmath
lam, f, s, ell, N = 632.8e-9, 0.500, 150.0e-6, 64, 57
R = s/(2*math.sin(math.pi/N))
def I(r): return abs(sum(cmath.exp(1j*(ell*2*math.pi*j/N - 2*math.pi*R*r*math.cos(2*math.pi*j/N)/(lam*f))) for j in range(N)))**2
r = 100e-6
while I(r + 1e-6) > I(r): r += 1e-6
print(r*1e3)   # 0.317 to 0.318 (mm)

The verification notes give the Bessel-function calculation with a table over the pinhole counts 6 to 125 and a script that counts the 57 pinholes from the image file. Sources: Berkhout and Beijersbergen, Phys. Rev. Lett. 101, 100801 (2008); Thide et al., Phys. Rev. Lett. 99, 087701 (2007).
