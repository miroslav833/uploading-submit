Key: 110 mm. It stands in the attached notes in the first section, output line 'KEY r_t = 108.67 mm', and in the second section as 'from the r_t* relation 108.7 mm'.

By hand: Azuma and Hoshino (Bull. JSME 27, 2739, 1984): Re = Q/(sqrt(CDH) nu); r_t* = (r_t/sqrt(CDH)) Re^(-1/3); r_t* falls as Re^(-2/3) from its largest value 0.61 at the critical Re = 7.4e4. Here sqrt(0.570 x 25.0 x 3.00) = 6.538 mm; Re = 1.48e-3/(6.538e-3 x 0.836e-6) = 2.708e5; Re/7.4e4 = 3.659; r_t* = 0.61 x 3.659^(-2/3) = 0.2569; Re^(1/3) = 64.69; r_t = 0.2569 x 6.538 x 64.69 = 108.7 mm. The paper's local Reynolds number relation (470 at the critical point, inversely proportional to r_t* squared) gives 111 mm, the same at two figures.

Most tempting wrong answer: 97 mm, from the round-jet correlation r_t/d = 1200 Re_d^-0.422 with d = 2 sqrt(CDH) (96.8 mm). Watson's radius 0.3155 sqrt(CDH) Re^(1/3), where the boundary layer reaches the surface, is 130 mm; a constant r_t* of 0.61 gives 260 mm; a constant local Reynolds number of 470 gives 600 mm.

Check program:
from math import sqrt
Q, nu, C, D, H = 1.48e-3, 0.836e-6, 0.570, 0.0250, 0.00300
a = sqrt(C*D*H); Re = Q/(a*nu)
print(0.61*(Re/7.4e4)**(-2/3)*a*Re**(1/3)*1e3)   # 108.7

Source: T. Azuma and T. Hoshino, Bull. JSME 27 (234), 2739-2746 (1984), Secs. 3, 5.1, 5.2, 5.3.
