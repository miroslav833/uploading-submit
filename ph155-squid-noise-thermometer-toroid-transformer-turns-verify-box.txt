The figure (300 x 300 px) is a simulated photograph of a ring-core transformer from above, and two integers are read from it. The core is a dark ring between 63 and 124 px from the image centre. The thin wire crosses it 52 times, evenly, over 300 degrees of the ring (51 gaps of about 5.9 degrees, about 12 px apart near the outer rim). The thick wire crosses it 5 times, at the bottom of the image. So Np = 52 and Ns = 5.

Key by hand: L_p = A_L Np^2 = 81.12 uH, L_s = 0.750 uH, M = A_L Np Ns = 7.800 uH. The closed superconducting secondary keeps its flux, M i_p + (L_s + L_i) i_s = 0, so i_s/i_p = 7.800/2.550 = 3.0588. Its screening current reduces the inductance of the primary loop to L_p - M^2/(L_s + L_i) = L_p L_i/(L_s + L_i) = 57.26 uH, so the current noise 4 kB T/R of the resistor rolls off at R/(2 pi L_eff) = 138.97 Hz, and at 400.0 Hz it is lower by 1 + (400/138.97)^2 = 9.284. The measured 30.20 uPhi0/rtHz times 0.2500 uA/Phi0 is 7.550 pA/rtHz in the input coil and 2.468 pA/rtHz in the primary loop. Then T = R S_I (1 + (f/f_c)^2)/(4 kB) = 0.05000 x (2.468e-12)^2 x 9.284/(4 x 1.380649e-23) = 0.0512 K, that is 51 mK.

The most tempting wrong answer is 97 mK, from a roll-off computed with the unscreened inductance L_p. Leaving the roll-off out gives 5.5 mK, the ideal turns ratio 4.4 mK, M/L_i without L_s 26 mK, and 51 or 53 primary turns 50 or 53 mK.

Check in a second (Python 3, standard library). It prints T in mK, then the value with the unscreened inductance:
import math
kB, R, AL, Li, c, S, f, Np, Ns = 1.380649e-23, 50.00e-3, 30.00e-9, 1.800e-6, 0.2500e-6, 30.20e-6, 400.0, 52, 5
Lp, Ls, M = AL*Np**2, AL*Ns**2, AL*Np*Ns
T = lambda L: R*(c*S*(Ls + Li)/M)**2*(1 + (2*math.pi*f*L/R)**2)/(4*kB)*1e3
print(round(T(Lp*Li/(Ls + Li)), 2), round(T(Lp), 1))   # 51.21 97.2
