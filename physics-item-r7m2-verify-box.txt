The figure is a simulated STM topograph (600 x 600 px). What is read from it is the angle between the straight edge of the film and the rows of atoms. The edge runs from (348, 0) px to (443, 600) px, 9.0 degrees from the vertical (99.0 degrees counter-clockwise from the image horizontal). The atoms form a square lattice of 8.6 px whose rows run at 37.7 and 127.7 degrees from the horizontal. For example, the atoms at (382, 494) px and (66, 85) px lie on one row of the second family, 60 spacings apart, 37.7 degrees from the vertical. The row family at 127.7 degrees is the one closest to the edge, so the edge makes 28.7 degrees with a row of nearest neighbours (and 61.3 degrees with the other family). A Fourier transform of the lattice and a straight-line fit of the edge give 28.7 degrees.

Key by hand. The rows are Cu-O bond directions, so the edge normal is at alpha = 28.7 degrees to a Cu-O bond. A quasiparticle at the angle phi to the normal sees the gaps D0 cos 2(phi - alpha) and D0 cos 2(phi + alpha) on the two legs of its trajectory. A zero-energy bound state exists when the two have opposite signs: 45 - alpha < |phi| < 45 + alpha degrees. In the momentum along the edge, k = k_F sin(phi), this is a range of width sqrt(2) k_F sin(alpha) for each sign. One state per 2 pi/L, per spin and per plane gives N/L = sqrt(2) k_F sin(alpha)/pi = 3.331 x 0.480 = 1.600, so 1.6 per nm. Angles from 27.7 to 29.7 degrees give 1.6.

The most tempting wrong answer is 2.0 per nm: the rows taken along the diagonals of the image, alpha = 36 degrees. Other values: rows taken along the image axes (9 degrees) 0.52; the fraction of directions 4 alpha/pi used without the projection on the edge 1.5; the angle taken from the nodal direction (16.3 degrees) 0.93; both spins counted 3.2; one sign of the momentum only 0.80; the angle to the other row family (61.3 degrees) 2.9; the two tilts added (43.3 degrees) 2.3; the factor sqrt(2) left out 1.1; without the figure, half of the channels k_F/(2 pi) 1.2, the (110) edge 2.4, an edge along (2, 1) 1.5 and along (3, 2) 1.8; alpha taken as 30 degrees 1.7.

Check in a few seconds (Python 3, standard library). The first number is the key, the second the most tempting wrong answer:
import math
f = lambda deg: math.sqrt(2)*7.40*math.sin(math.radians(deg))/math.pi
print(f(28.7))    # 1.600
print(f(36.0))    # 1.958
