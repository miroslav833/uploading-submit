What the figure carries, as text. The frame is 240 x 240 px. It shows 61 spots on a circle of radius 84.0 px about the point (121.8, 117.8) px, one spot per ion, 8.65 px apart along the circle. Measured counter-clockwise from the +x direction of the image the spots are at 1.8, 7.7, 13.7, 19.6, 25.4, 31.3 degrees and so on in steps of 360/61 = 5.90 degrees up to 355.8 degrees. By quadrant about the ring centre: 15, 16, 15, 15. Counted counter-clockwise from the spot just above three o'clock, spot 1 is at (205.7, 115.1) px, spot 11 at (162.8, 44.5), spot 21 at (80.0, 44.9), spot 31 at (37.8, 116.2), spot 41 at (77.3, 188.9), spot 51 at (159.9, 192.7) and spot 61 at (205.6, 124.0), from the read-back script in the notes. The spots near the axis of the cooling beam are a little brighter than the others; none is missing. No scale is printed, because the ring radius is given in the question.

Two checks of the count that need no tally. The distance from a spot to the tenth spot after it is 82.7 px and the ring diameter is 168.0 px, so ten spacings subtend 2 arcsin(82.7/168.0) = 59.0 degrees and the ring holds 3600/59.0 = 61.0 spots (60 spots would give 84.0 px, 62 spots 81.5 px). The point opposite a spot lies midway between two spots, which shows that the number is odd.

The answer by hand. N = 61, so d = 2 pi R/N = 2 pi x 148.5 um/61 = 15.296 um. For out-of-plane displacements z_n ~ exp(i p n) the Coulomb repulsion gives f(p)^2 = fz^2 - k' F(p), with k' = K/(4 pi^2 m d^3) = 2.3071e-28/(4 pi^2 x 6.6354e-26 kg x 3.5788e-15 m^3) = 2.4610e10 Hz^2 and F(p) = 2 sum_k (1 - cos pk)/k^3. Near p = pi, F(pi - q) = (7/2) zeta(3) - q^2 ln 2 = 4.2072 - 0.6931 q^2. The ring closes only for p = 2 pi j/61. The alternating pattern p = pi is not among these, because 61 is odd. Lowest: j = 30, 31, q = pi/61, f1^2 = 1.2250e11 - 2.4610e10 x 4.20536 = 1.9006e10 Hz^2, f1 = 137.86 kHz. Next: j = 29, 32, q = 3 pi/61, f2^2 = 1.2250e11 - 2.4610e10 x 4.19065 = 1.9368e10 Hz^2, f2 = 139.17 kHz. Difference 1.31 kHz. With the exact chord lengths 2 R sin(pi k/61) in place of k d: 136.73 and 138.05 kHz, difference 1.317 kHz. Answer 1.3 kHz.

The most tempting wrong answer is 1.8 kHz. It belongs to 64 ions, the largest number for which the ring is stable with these givens (65 ions would buckle out of the plane): the lowest mode is then at 50.90 kHz and the next at 52.74 kHz, difference 1.83 kHz. A spacing measured only 5 per cent short, 8.25 px for 8.65 px, leads to that number. A second wrong answer, 0.66 kHz, treats the 61 ions as an even ring: lowest mode at p = pi, f = sqrt(1.2250e11 - 2.4610e10 x 4.20720) = 137.70 kHz, next mode at q = 2 pi/61, f = 138.35 kHz. Other wrong results: 60 ions give 0.58 kHz, 62 ions 0.79 kHz, 59 ions 1.0 kHz, 63 ions 2.0 kHz; nearest neighbours only give 1.7 kHz.

A check in a few lines of Python (standard library), with the exact sum over the ring:

import math
K, m, R, fz, N = 2.3071e-28, 39.96*1.6605e-27, 148.5e-6, 350.0e3, 61
f = sorted(math.sqrt((2*math.pi*fz)**2 - K/m*sum((1 - math.cos(2*math.pi*j*k/N))/(2*R*math.sin(math.pi*k/N))**3 for k in range(1, N)))/(2*math.pi) for j in range(N))
print(f[0], f[2], f[2] - f[0])    # 136729 Hz, 138046 Hz, 1317 Hz (f[0] = f[1] and f[2] = f[3] are degenerate pairs)

The count can be checked from the image without counting by eye: the brightness along the circle has its strongest Fourier component at order 61 (script in the attached notes). The method follows Fishman, De Chiara, Calarco and Morigi, Phys. Rev. B 77, 064111 (2008); ion rings of this kind are described by Tabakov et al., Phys. Rev. Applied 4, 031001 (2015) and Li et al., Phys. Rev. Lett. 118, 053001 (2017).
